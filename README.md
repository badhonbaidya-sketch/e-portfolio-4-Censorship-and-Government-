## Artefact 1: Government Report – Age Assurance Technology Trial (2025)

**Link:** https://www.infrastructure.gov.au/department/media/publications/age-assurance-technology-trial-final-report

![Age Assurance Technology Trial final report](/artefact1.png)

*Source: Age Check Certification Scheme (2025)*

### Summary of the artefact
The Government's Age Assurance Technology Trial is the final report which was released in September 2025 before the under-16 age ban came into effect. It evaluated over 60 age check tools from 48 vendors and discovered that age checks are not only possible but there's a "grey zone" that for facial age estimation is around 2-3 years either side of the limit. Other providers stored more personal information than required (Age Check Certification Scheme 2025).

### Justification on why I chose the artefact
I'm studying a Bachelor of IT with a major in software development, and the laws, such as, the under-16 rule, are implemented via software. The age-checks, content filters and geo-blocks are developed by the developers. This report supports two dilemmas that I might encounter in my career. The first is what is known as accuracy; the accuracy of facial age estimation is poorest at the ages of interest like 15 vs. 16, and real users are wrongly blocked. The other is privacy: adults will probably have to submit a facial scan or ID, and developers will be required to store and secure that information (Age Check Certification Scheme 2025). I'm sort of less happy about the ban, because many people will be "wrong" in the 2 to 3 year "grey zone" near 16.

## Artefact 2: Scholarly Article – O'Donnell (2025), *Alternative Law Journal*

**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1177/1037969X251394092

![O'Donnell (2025) article in Alternative Law Journal](/artefact2.png)

*Source: O'Donnell (2025)*

### Summary of the artefact
O'Donnell explains how the *Online Safety Amendment (Social Media Minimum Age) Act 2024* works. Platforms must be able to reasonably prevent children under 16 from being on their service and the penalties are up to $49.5 million per violation, but "there is no penalty if youth are successful in decepting platforms to open an account" (O'Donnell 2025, p. 305–306). Platforms should also give users a choice of not using government ID, and they should remove all data upon use.

### Justification on why I chose the artefact
This article was chosen because it exemplifies the actual application of the law. The requirement doesn't fall on users, it falls on the platforms, as it does with Section 313 where carriers are required to assist authorities in order to enforce the law (CQUniversity 2026). It also demonstrates the importance of supervision. According to me, Parliament should draw the line, the court should check it and the regulators should be transparent, rather than one agency, or one company, doing it. The harm principle of Mill is that it is only necessary to protect against harm to other people (Quinn 2020), so measures such as the destruction of ID data can be of utmost importance. I believe this is too ambiguous for a developer to build to: no indication of which checks to use, or how many errors are acceptable, so all platforms are essentially "guessing" what it means.

## Artefact 3: News Article – ABC News (10 December 2025)

**Link:** https://www.abc.net.au/news/2025-12-10/social-media-ban-day-one-teen-access/106126706

![ABC News article on age verification errors](/artefact3.png)

*Source: Dervisevic (2025), ABC News*

### Summary of the artefact
Here's a first day of the ban ABC article which demonstrated how easy the age checks were to fool. A 14-year-old died as 23 on Snapchat, a 13-year-old died Instagram photo check as 16 and other teens did the face scan on their peers or used VPNs or even makeup. While teens who skirted the ban on day one may not avoid it "in weeks or months" (Dervisevic 2025), the Communications Minister said.

### Justification on why I chose the artefact
I'm most curious about the use of VPNs and Tor to get around blocks and how effective blocking is. This article indicated that the age checks were broken after day one anyway. During the class discussion, some shared the opinion that VPNs and Tor make blocking irrelevant, and others suggested that the majority of people don't attempt to circumvent the blocking, so that is why it can still be helpful in minimising harm. It also connects with the slides from the workshop about the limitations of DNS and IP blocking (CQUniversity 2026). But I'd be slightly in the second camp: kids will find a way around, but most won't bother, so it still has some effect, though not as much as the government suggests.

## Artefact 4: Workshop Personal Reflection

**Workshop:** Week 9, Thursday, 17 September 2026, Mr Umapathy Venugopal, CQU Sydney

![Selfie in the Week 9 Censorship and Government workshop](/week9-selfie.jpeg)

*My selfie from the Week 9 workshop with my classmates, my tutor Mr Umapathy Venugopal and the workshop slides in the background.*

### Summary of the artefact: My Personal Reflection
That really made me think and analyze was the case study where ASIC blocked an IP address instead of the domain names (House of Representatives Standing Committee on Infrastructure and Communications 2015) which ended up blocking approximately 250,000 legitimate websites including Melbourne Free University. The one thing that I didn't like was the lack of transparency. No court was involved and it wasn't until June that the media was told ASIC was behind it. To me, Cloudflare removing the Daily Stormer was a big deal too. I believe it was the correct decision, but the CEO said it would be more difficult to refuse requests to take them down from government later (Prince 2017).

### Justification on why I chose the artefact
In the discussion, I said that agencies indeed need blocking powers, but if there's a check on it, as there is for copyright blocking which is done by the court. When protection becomes silencing of criticism, for me, it's restriction. The 26 platforms were banned in Nepal, and that ban crossed that line, leading to the Gen Z protests (CQUniversity, 2026). One country should not be in charge of the rest of the world's content (Evans & Butler 2024), as demonstrated by the eSafety v X case. I see myself as a future developer, and if my code makes the wrong decision to block someone or to be used to shut down people, as part of the developer's responsibility.As a future developer, I understand that if it happens that my code makes an incorrect decision to block someone, or it is used someone else to block someone, part of my responsibility as a developer is to take that one. (Australian Computer Society 2023)
